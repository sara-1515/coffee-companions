The Rich History & Origins of Coffee

Coffee Through The Ages

850 CE

The Legend of Kaldi

According to Ethiopian legend, a goat herder named Kaldi discovered coffee after noticing his goats became energetic after eating berries from a certain tree. Kaldi reported his findings to the local monastery, where monks made a drink with the berries, allowing them to stay awake during long hours of prayer.

1400s

Coffee Cultivation in Yemen

Coffee cultivation and trade began on the Arabian Peninsula. By the 15th century, coffee was being grown in the Yemeni district of Arabia, and by the 16th century, it was known in Persia, Egypt, Syria, and Turkey.

1600s

European Coffee Houses

Coffee made its way to Europe and coffeehouses became centers of social activity and communication. In major cities like Venice, London, and Paris, these establishments were nicknamed "penny universities" because patrons could purchase a cup of coffee for a penny and engage in stimulating conversation.

1700s

Global Expansion

Coffee plants spread to the New World through colonial powers. The French introduced coffee to the Caribbean, the Spanish to Central America, and the Portuguese to Brazil. Coffee plantations expanded rapidly as demand increased throughout Europe and America.

1800s

Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution brought innovations in coffee brewing and processing. Various patents for coffee roasting machines, espresso machines, and vacuum coffee makers were filed during this period, making coffee more accessible to the masses.

1900s to Present

Modern Coffee Culture

The 20th century saw the rise of instant coffee, commercial coffee chains, and specialty coffee movements. Today, coffee is the second most traded commodity after oil, with millions of people worldwide involved in growing, processing, trading, and preparing coffee.

Major Coffee Growing Regions

Ethiopia

The birthplace of coffee, Ethiopia produces some of the world's most distinctive and highly valued coffees with extraordinary diversity of flavors.

Flavor Profile

  • Bright fruit notes (blueberry, strawberry)
  • Floral aromatics (jasmine, bergamot)
  • Wine-like acidity
  • Medium body with honey sweetness

Colombia

Colombia's geography provides ideal growing conditions, producing consistently excellent coffee known for its rich flavor and medium acidity.

Flavor Profile

  • Caramel sweetness
  • Nutty undertones (walnut, hazelnut)
  • Chocolate notes
  • Clean, balanced finish

Central America

Including Guatemala, Costa Rica, and Honduras, this region produces bright, clean coffees with distinctive regional characteristics.

Flavor Profile

  • Bright citrus acidity
  • Apple and pear notes
  • Cinnamon and spice undertones
  • Clean, crisp finish

Brazil

The world's largest coffee producer, Brazil is known for its low-acidity coffees with nutty, chocolate notes and full bodies.

Flavor Profile

  • Pronounced nuttiness
  • Milk chocolate sweetness
  • Low acidity
  • Full, creamy body

Indonesia

Indonesian islands like Sumatra, Java, and Sulawesi produce distinct coffees with earthy, spicy profiles and full bodies.

Flavor Profile

  • Earthy, woody notes
  • Herbal and spice characters
  • Low acidity
  • Full, syrupy body

Yemen

One of the oldest coffee-growing regions, Yemen produces distinctive, complex coffees with a unique dry processing method.

Flavor Profile

  • Dried fruit notes (date, fig)
  • Spicy undertones (cardamom, cinnamon)
  • Wine-like complexity
  • Rustic, wild character

Coffee Fun Facts

Coffee is the second most traded commodity in the world, following only crude oil.

It takes about 37 gallons of water to grow enough coffee beans for one cup of coffee.

Moderate coffee consumption (3-5 cups daily) has been linked to a longer lifespan and reduced risk of several diseases.

There are two main species of coffee plant: Arabica (higher quality) and Robusta (higher caffeine).
